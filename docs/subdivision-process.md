# How a residential subdivision is made and sold

A reading of *A Guide to Understanding Residential Subdivisions in California* (Esquivel and Alvayay, California Department of Real Estate and CSU Sacramento, 2014), which sits on the [reference shelf](reference-shelf.md). It explains the processes behind a developer's phase, public report, and hand-over of a common interest development to its association. It is a map for reading the developer file, not the law: the guide is from 2014, so a section it cites is read from the current text (`jason authorities`) before it is relied on, and nothing here is recited as the statute's words.

Page references are the guide's printed page numbers ("p. 56"). `jason reference --page` counts PDF pages, which are the printed number plus 12.

## Two laws, one order

| | Subdivision Map Act | Subdivided Lands Act (SLA) |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Government Code 66410-66499.58 (p. 47) | Business and Professions Code 11000-11200 (p. 54) |
| Purpose | the legal means to divide land and keep title sound (p. 54) | consumer protection: disclosure to buyers (p. 55) |
| Who decides | the city or county (p. 54) | the Real Estate Commissioner, through the Department (p. 54) |
| Result | a recorded map | a public report |
| Scope | every subdivision, residential or not, except undivided interests (p. 54) | residential subdivisions of five or more interests (p. 56) |

Compliance with the Map Act always comes first: the subdivision is recorded before a final public report issues and a binding sale is signed (p. 54). The Department "does not exercise land use authority" (p. 9) and does not run an operating association: its involvement begins with the application for a public report and ends when the last lot or unit is conveyed (p. 9). A homeowner's dispute with an operating association, or an assessment delinquency, is outside its jurisdiction (p. 10).

## The development sequence

The guide's three stages (Figure 8, p. 36), in the order the documents appear in a file:

1. **Predevelopment.** Feasibility; land purchase under a feasibility (due diligence) period; the **tentative map** and land use entitlements (a discretionary approval, with its conditions of approval); satisfying the conditions; approval of the **subdivision improvement plans**; the **final map** (a ministerial approval, then recording with the County Recorder, which "perfects" the subdivision) (pp. 41-43).
2. **Development and construction.** Site improvements; building permits; model homes; **formation of the association** and its governing documents; preliminary marketing (pp. 43-44).
3. **Selling.** Public report issued; sales contracts; occupancy; closings; the association begins to operate; warranty (pp. 44-45).

Public improvements are rarely finished before the final map. The Map Act lets the local agency accept a payment and performance bond or other security and record the map first (pp. 43, 50). The developer's construction loan is secured by a blanket deed of trust that must be released lot by lot at closing (p. 63).

## What kind of subdivision it is

A standard subdivision has wholly divided interests; a **common interest development** (CID) adds an undivided interest or a right of exclusive use (pp. 2, 14). The kinds, by the Davis-Stirling Act's definitions as the guide reads them:

- **Planned development**: common area owned by an association or by the owners in common, or an association that maintains common area and may lien for assessments (pp. 22-23, 50). The most common kind the Department reviewed from 1993 to 2012: 58 percent, against 32 percent condominiums and 10 percent standard subdivisions (p. 21).
- **Condominium**: a separate interest (a three-dimensional unit on a recorded plan) plus an undivided interest in the common area (p. 23). A *detached* or *site* condominium looks like a single-family subdivision and is chosen partly because a recorded condominium plan can replace a second map process (p. 24).
- **Community apartment** and **stock cooperative**: exclusive occupancy with title held in one entity, not separate deeds (pp. 23-25).
- **Undivided interest** (tenancy in common) subdivisions of five or more interests: under the SLA, outside the Map Act, and not CIDs (p. 26).

"Townhome" and "PUD" are building styles and zoning labels, not statutory types (p. 16). Common area for a condominium is often split into association-owned area and a "cloud" of airspace owned in common, which keeps direct liability on the association rather than the owners (p. 15).

## Is a public report required?

Figure 10 (p. 57) walks it: a residential project of five or more interests needs a report unless an exemption applies (p. 56). The exemptions listed: fewer than five interests; very large parcels; commercial or industrial limits by zoning or recorded declaration; public agency offerings; non-binding conversion expressions of intent; bulk sales between developers; certain undivided interests; limited equity cooperatives; timeshares. A **standard** subdivision in a city is exempt if every lot is sold with a completed home, the city holds adequate security for the public improvements, and buyer money is handled under 11013.2 or 11013.4 (pp. 21, 56).

## The public report

| Report | Paper | Use | Term |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preliminary | pink | nonbinding reservations before the final | one year (p. 58) |
| Conditional | yellow | binding contracts, with the final required before closing | six months, one six-month renewal (p. 58) |
| Final | white | the sale itself | five years, renewable in five-year periods (pp. 56, 69) |
| Amended, renewed, interim | | a material change (10 CCR 2800 lists some) or an expiry; an interim report allows nonbinding reservations meanwhile (p. 58) |

A buyer is given the report and must acknowledge receipt in writing before an offer is taken or money is accepted (p. 70). A material change to the offering, which can include a change to the governing documents while the developer is still involved, must be notified to the Department and may end the report (p. 69).

**The application** is the notice of intention: RE 624 for CIDs, RE 628 for standard subdivisions, in three parts (p. 62). The Department's review is quantitative (is the filing complete) then qualitative (BPC 11018.5; pp. 62-63). It must find reasonable arrangements for: completion of the subdivision, offsite improvements, and common areas; documents adequate to convey the interests; governing documents binding on every lot after the first conveyance; delivery of control to the purchasers; and management and maintenance of each purchaser's interest (pp. 62-63). The statutory processing clocks (Figure 14, p. 69):

| Final report | Quantitative | Qualitative | Response to corrections | Perfected file |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standard subdivision | 10 days | 20 days | 20 days | 10 days |
| CID | 10 days | 60 days | 30 days | 15 days |

## The association during the developer's control

The Department's review of a CID's association covers five things (p. 65): the **budget** (standardized forms, per phase, "the most important information" in the report), **special maintenance arrangements**, the **CC&Rs**, the **governing documents**, and **annexation**. The points that recur in a developer file:

- **Budget categories**: fixed costs, operating, reserves, administration, contingency (p. 29). The Department publishes an operating cost manual and reserve study guidelines (p. 30).
- **Assurance of the developer's share.** The developer pays assessments on unsold lots from the first conveyance of a phase (p. 66). The Department wants assurance of the money: a presale requirement, a security bond or other cash-convertible security for a number of months of assessments, or, for larger projects, a **subsidy agreement** (p. 65). A maintenance agreement is a different promise: the developer maintains part of the project in lieu of assessment and owners share any savings equally (p. 64). The securities themselves are in [developer-securities.md](developer-securities.md); the guide cites regulation 2792.9 for the assessment security (pp. 32, 65).
- **Completion of common improvements.** Finished before the report: delivered free of mechanics liens. Unfinished: a reasonable time and completion security, a bond the Department may accept (pp. 9, 64).
- **Transfer of common area** to the association, or to a trust for later conveyance, before the first conveyance (p. 66).
- **CC&Rs** recorded before the report issues, not subordinate to any lien at the first conveyance, with a mortgagee protection clause subordinating the assessment lien to a first mortgage (pp. 65-66). Foreclosure by a first lender extinguishes the lien but not the CC&Rs, and the association may seek a personal judgment (pp. 29, 66).
- **Assessments.** Straight allocation (total budget over the number of lots) unless benefit differs by more than 10 percent; regular assessments commence at the first conveyance; the board may not raise them more than 20 percent, or levy special assessments over 5 percent of the budgeted gross expenses, without a majority of the members other than the developer (p. 66). The guide gives these as what the Department looks for in the documents; the Davis-Stirling Act sets the rules for an operating association.
- **Governance.** One vote per lot or unit, with an optional Class B for the developer (three votes per interest, ending at 75 percent sold); a member election within six months of the first conveyance; a first annual meeting within 45 days of 51 percent of the first phase closing or six months, whichever is earlier; the budget distributed 45 days before the fiscal year and an annual report within 120 days after it (pp. 28, 67). Prohibited provisions include contracts over a year, capital spending or sales over 5 percent of budgeted gross expenditures, and compensation of directors (p. 67; 10 CCR 2792.21(b)).
- **Amendments**: a majority of the members other than the developer, and never less than the approval the original clause required (p. 68).
- **Architectural control**: the developer may appoint the first committee for one year; after that the board appoints at least one member until 90 percent of interests are conveyed (p. 68).
- **Phasing and annexation.** Annexation needs two thirds of the non-developer members unless the Department approved an annexation plan in a master file; each annexed phase has its own report; the plan shows the common area is not overburdened and assessments will not materially exceed those disclosed (p. 68). A phased project's budget is stated per phase (p. 65).
- **Warranties and guaranties.** A promise that cannot be performed before closing, such as the developer's subsidy, needs financial arrangements (p. 68). The guide notes separately the Right to Repair Act (Civil Code 895-945.5), with its 10-year latent and 4-year patent periods (pp. 20-21).

## What the Department no longer touches

Once sales are over and the developer's membership ends, the Department does not mediate disputes, set a budget, or step between an association and its vendors or surety (pp. 9-10). The guide's own account of the 2007-2009 downturn is why the assessment security exists: unsold units, rentals above lender thresholds, and liens extinguished by lender foreclosure left associations short (pp. 31-33).

## Using this

- **Reading a developer file.** Match each document to its step: recorded map, conditions of approval and improvement security (Map Act); notice of intention, public reports, and the securities (SLA). A missing step is a question for the developer file, not a conclusion.
- **Age.** Before reciting any section, a requirement, a figure, or a form number from this page, check it against the current statute or regulation on the authorities shelf and the Department's current forms. The guide dates the Davis-Stirling Act's recodification (effective January 1, 2014) but not what followed.
- **Where the law and the guide differ,** the law governs, and a difference is a finding for the board, not an edit here.
